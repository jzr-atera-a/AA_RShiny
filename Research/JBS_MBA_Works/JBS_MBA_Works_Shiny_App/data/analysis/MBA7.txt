@OVERVIEW
The paper argues that the hard part of AI transformation in retail banking is not the technology or the budget but the organisation's culture. Drawing on the author's experience as Head of AI and Digital Transformation at a large UK bank, it treats the transformation as a cultural problem first. It applies two course frameworks in sequence: a three-level model of culture (artifacts, espoused values and underlying assumptions) to diagnose the bank, and a change model (shared need, vision, commitment, monitoring and sustaining change) to prescribe a route through it.

@BLOCK Diagnosing the culture: what the bank shows and what it says|Three levels of culture
The author sorts the bank's culture into three layers. Visible artifacts include specialist AI teams, code repositories, progress meetings and performance dashboards. Espoused values are the aspirations leaders state, such as a general AI strategy, customer-satisfaction goals and regulatory guidance, and the paper observes that these were not always matched by practice. The deepest layer, underlying assumptions, is where the most resistance sits: a unionised workforce, a perception that digital transformation was mainly branding, and a fear that AI will cost jobs after branch closures. The diagnosis is useful because it explains why the bank's stated strategy could be sound and still fail. The gap between the layers is where most of the paper's practical argument lies.

@BLOCK Creating the need and shaping a vision|Change framework, stages one and two
The change model begins by making the case that the bank must thrive in a competitive, disrupting market, and that digital innovation should be presented to executives as creative destruction rather than as a cost. The vision stage relies on regulation: the author describes how Open Banking forced the bank to share data and launch new services, and offers Deutsche Bank's payments work as a comparison. The paper also borrows the idea of a "massive transformative purpose", a short aspirational statement that gives employees something to organise around. The example it proposes, a promise of a customised digital experience, is plausible but generic, and the paper does not show how such a statement would be tested against the bank's actual operations.

@BLOCK Commitment, monitoring and making the change last|Mobilising commitment and sustaining change
Commitment is built through a clear problem definition, public objectives and a culture that tolerates failure. The author uses examples from Google, Intel and the Navy SEALs to make the point, although the mechanisms are asserted rather than shown in the bank. Progress is to be tracked with objectives and key results, for example a ten per cent annual revenue increase from AI. The paper's most concrete point about sustaining change is the value of a central AI council that gives a single plan and reduces the silos that divided specialists, IT and business units. The conclusion stresses training, leadership alignment and clear communication about how AI changes roles, and it returns to the three-level model to suggest that artifacts change first but assumptions change only with time and consistent behaviour.

@CRITIQUE
The paper's main strength is that it takes the cultural argument seriously and makes it operational through two frameworks. Its main weakness is evidence. The case rests almost entirely on the author's memory of one bank, which is not named, and on secondary examples from other companies, so the causal claims about culture are plausible rather than demonstrated. The "underlying assumptions" section is incomplete in the text, with its bullet points left blank, and the paper includes an AI-generated passage quoted as if it were analysis, which weakens its authority. The advice is consistent with the course literature, but the paper would be more persuasive if it showed how it measured culture change, for example through survey data before and after the programme, or identified which intervention actually changed the underlying assumptions.

@LINKS
The paper shows the course's organisational behaviour material applied to a sector where regulation, legacy structure and public trust all constrain change. Its most transferable insight is that leaders who announce an AI strategy without addressing the assumptions of the people who must deliver it are likely to see the strategy stall, however good the technology.
