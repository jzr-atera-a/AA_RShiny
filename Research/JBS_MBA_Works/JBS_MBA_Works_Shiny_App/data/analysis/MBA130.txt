@OVERVIEW
This op-ed, written as a response to the question "Are emerging market currencies more a risk or an opportunity?", argues that emerging market currencies are now a strategic opportunity rather than a risk, and that banks and investors who use modern analytics can see the value others miss. It draws on auction theory, on Amartya Sen's concept of development as capabilities, on an AI-based forecasting tool used by a Latin American central bank, and on IMF and Bank of England material. The piece ends with a call to financial institutions to build regional expertise and use AI-powered risk models. A companion interactive dashboard, not reviewed here in detail, is offered as supporting analysis.

@BLOCK Reframing the question as one of evaluation|The opportunity argument
The op-ed's central move is to say that the real question is not whether these currencies are risky but whether investors can evaluate that risk well. The claim that outperformance has been seen in several emerging currencies, while investors have pulled back from them, is an interesting observation that the piece uses to make its case. The argument that institutional holdings have fallen, from 8 per cent of global equity fund holdings in 2017 to 5 per cent, is presented as evidence of mispricing caused by hesitation. That interpretation is possible, but a fall in allocation can equally reflect weaker returns or higher risk. The op-ed does not show which explanation fits the data.

@BLOCK Auction theory, development and the case for AI|Theory and the evidence for AI
The piece uses the work of two Nobel laureates on auctions and private information as a lens for why some investors see value that others do not. The link is metaphorical rather than demonstrated: auction theory explains how bidders with private information behave, and does not establish that a currency is undervalued. Sen's framing, that development should be measured by capabilities, supports the claim that emerging economies have strengthened institutions. The AI evidence is the weakest. The op-ed cites a central bank's use of AI forecasting to show that analytics improve decisions, but the research it cites is a study of how difficult currency forecasting is, and it finds that a simple random-walk model with a drift is a strong benchmark. Presenting that paper as evidence that AI improves forecasting reverses its main message.

@BLOCK Risks acknowledged and the regional case|Balancing risk and reward
The piece does acknowledge risk. It cites a Bank of England warning on volatility, and an IMF figure showing emerging currencies falling about 4 per cent against the dollar, while noting that averages hide large differences between countries. It then gives a practical example of a UK business that borrows in the local currency of a supplier to match debts with costs, which is a sound hedging logic. The caveat about averages is the right point to make, but the piece does not say which countries are in the strong group, or how an investor would separate them. Growth forecasts of about 4 per cent for emerging economies are used to argue that the markets are stable. Growth and currency stability are different things, and the piece treats them as one.

@BLOCK A call to action|Conclusion and recommendations
The conclusion asks financial institutions to abandon the view that emerging currencies are inherently risky, invest in regional expertise and use AI-based risk models. The recommendations are clear and fit the question. Their force depends on the earlier evidence, which the piece presents with more confidence than it can carry. The phrase "increasingly stable assets" is a strong claim, supported by one newspaper article and by macroeconomic forecasts rather than by a measure of currency volatility.

@CRITIQUE
The op-ed is persuasive in tone and clearly organised, and it answers the question with a reasoned position rather than a list. Its weaknesses lie in the evidence. The auction-theory argument is metaphorical and does not show mispricing. The AI example is misread: the cited study finds that currency forecasting is hard and that a simple benchmark is strong, which argues against the claim it is used to support. The allocation fall is interpreted as hesitation without ruling out weaker returns. The growth forecasts are used as a proxy for currency stability, and the 2024 figure is a forecast for a year that had already passed when the piece was written, so it should be presented as an outturn or dropped. The dashboard is an appropriate supporting tool, but the op-ed should not rely on it for claims it cannot show. A stronger version would test the opportunity claim against currency volatility and drawdown data, separate the strong from the weak emerging currencies by a stated rule, and describe the AI evidence accurately, including its limits.

@LINKS
The op-ed applies the course's international finance material, on exchange rate risk, hedging and the drivers of capital flows, to a live question about emerging markets. It shows that a policy argument about risk and reward depends on whether the evidence measures the same thing as the claim, and that a hedging example is only as convincing as the volatility data behind it.
