@OVERVIEW
This individual assignment takes Meta's AI strategy, as presented by another study group in the final Strategy session (Strategy-40), and tests it against two course frameworks, VRIO and network effects, before giving an overall verdict using Rumelt's test for a good strategy. The author accepts the group's diagnosis, that Meta's advantage lies in its user data and scale, and asks whether the proposed actions follow from it. The conclusion is that the strategy is of medium strength: a clear diagnosis, but thin on implementation and on regulatory risk.

@BLOCK Setting out the strategy under test|Overview of Strategy-40
The strategy is summarised as a plan to invest heavily in AI infrastructure, to use Meta's open-weight Llama models to commoditise generative AI while building specialist tools for advertising and content creation, to extend into new platforms, and to fund the programme from cash, debt and asset sales. The author picks out the central idea: open-weight models are a deliberate difference from competitors, and the value comes from proprietary data used to train models for advertising and recommendation. This is a useful framing, but the paper treats the plan's scale, including the investment figures, as given rather than as a claim to test.

@BLOCK Value, rarity, imitability and organisation|VRIO assessment
Under value, the paper finds the strategy's description of Meta as a "merchant for human attention" over-simple, and credits Llama with widening access and improving recommendations. Rarity rests on Meta's large user datasets, which the author sees as hard for smaller rivals to match. Imitability is the weakest part of the argument. The author notes that Google follows a similar embedded approach, that DeepSeek could build on the open-weight models, and that a performance claim is not supported by the data shown. Organisation is judged by how Meta integrates AI across its platforms, but the paper notes an apparent inconsistency between investment in cloud and in its own computing. The VRIO discussion identifies where the advantage could be lost, which is more useful than a simple pass or fail.

@BLOCK Network effects and the ecosystem around users|Direct and indirect network effects
The network effects section argues that engagement raises platform value directly, and that advertisers, developers and creators add value indirectly. Meta's roughly 3.4 billion daily users are presented as the core asset. The main criticism is that the strategy describes the ecosystem in terms of what it is rather than how it will be grown or governed: investment in talent and data centres is described superficially, the measures of user access and time spent are thin, and relations with regulators are touched on lightly. The suggestion that objectives and key results would make growth measurable is a useful practical point.

@BLOCK Judging the strategy against Rumelt's test|Overall assessment
The verdict is that Meta has moderate strength in VRIO and network effects, with real strengths in data and AI-driven personalisation, but execution gaps in regulation, retention and infrastructure. Against Rumelt's criteria the strategy has a clear diagnosis but lacks detail on implementation. The author also sets it against Thiel's ideas on breakthrough innovation and the blue ocean approach, and finds that the metaverse and open-source AI are plausible uncontested spaces, although competitors are closing in. The conclusion is cautious: competitor adaptability challenges any claim to uniqueness.

@CRITIQUE
The paper is well structured and applies both frameworks in a disciplined way. Its best quality is candour about the weaknesses of the group's presentation, which it tests rather than repeats. Its main limitation is that it judges the strategy largely against its own figures and slides, and does not test it against market evidence, such as how quickly competitors close the performance gap. The verdict of medium strength is sensible but is not derived from a stated scoring method. The claim that the metaverse is a blue ocean is asserted rather than shown, since the paper does not establish that the market is uncontested. Some sections, particularly on rarity and network effects, drift into general description. The clearest improvement would be to score each VRIO element and show the evidence behind each score, and to ask which of the strategy's assumptions would have to fail for the verdict to change.

@LINKS
The paper is a practical example of applying VRIO and network effects to a live strategy, and of using Rumelt's test to judge whether a strategy is a diagnosis with coherent action rather than a vision. It shows that competitive advantage in platforms depends on imitability and on the ability to keep users, not only on scale.
