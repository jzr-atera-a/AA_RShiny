@OVERVIEW
This diary-style individual assessment reflects on a series of negotiation exercises from the Negotiations Lab, covering the Salt Harbor, MedLee, PowerScreen, Grand Afroport and World Trade Center cases, together with two plenary sessions. It is organised around the strategies the author saw work, followed by lessons about himself and about others. The central message is that preparation, framing, identity and emotional intelligence matter more than tactics, and that power has to be used with care.

@BLOCK Strategies that worked in practice|Interests, framing and emotion
The author's core strategy is to negotiate on interests rather than positions. This worked in the Salt Harbor development deal, where incremental concessions satisfied both sides. In MedLee, a joint venture across cultures, the team relied on reframing and empathy, and reached a result by treating the family's identity as something to preserve. The PowerScreen dispute showed that emotions can override the commercial terms, and the author's response was to address recognition before money, which kept both the company and the relationship. Each case shows the same pattern: the tactic that succeeds is the one that identifies what each party needs to protect.

@BLOCK Coalitions and the emotional difficulty of WTC|Coalitions and the most demanding negotiation
The Grand Afroport negotiation required a public-private partnership, and the author's team built a coalition of three parties that isolated a less cooperative player. The result shows that coalitions can make mutually beneficial deals easier, at the cost of excluding someone who pursued only their top demands. The WTC reconstruction was the most emotionally charged exercise, because one player set aside their role and argued from a nationalistic position. The author describes listening, acknowledging the feeling, and then pulling the discussion back to the role and to interests. He says the negotiation turned when the group separated preferable outcomes from unacceptable ones.

@BLOCK What the author learned about his own negotiating|Self-learning: preparation, assumptions and framing
The reflections are the most personal part. Preparation is presented as the foundation of any talent, and the author notes that underperforming peers relied on generative AI to summarise their instructions, which undermined their learning. He admits that the PowerScreen video led him to assume Hacker was reactive, and that understanding the manager's psychology removed that assumption. He also reports that presenting his positions too directly early on was a mistake, and that the sequence and context of options matter. The 70:30 rule, which lets the counterpart talk more, and the recognition that his role as the bank gave him a veto, are useful practical points. The source for the 70:30 rule is a hostage-negotiation reference that is only loosely connected to commercial settings.

@BLOCK Lessons from peers and from the plenary sessions|Learning from others, identity and power
The author draws lessons from the group: that a saboteur can hide among participants, that identity must be addressed before terms can change, and that a coalition can be built by first listening to the parties who gain most from one's own role. The plenary sessions add a different perspective. The astronaut speaker is used to show that clear, factual communication matters in high-stakes teams, and the Brexit session is used to argue that a leader who fails to plan for contingencies and does not take ownership of a poor outcome damages the result. The author's reading of the Brexit failure as one of framing and planning is reasonable, but it leaves out the structural constraints the speaker described.

@CRITIQUE
The diary has real insight. It turns the cases into a set of testable lessons, and its best passages are candid about the author's early mistakes, such as stating positions too directly, and about the difficulty of responding to an emotional remark in a live exercise. The main weakness is that the lessons are often stated with more confidence than the evidence allows. Several rest on a single exercise or an anecdote, and some ideas borrowed from the reading list are attached to claims the readings do not quite make, such as the use of the 70:30 rule from hostage negotiation as general advice. The Brexit session is treated as a clear case of failure without testing that judgement against the constraints the speaker raised. The outcome of each negotiation is described but rarely compared with what a better move would have achieved, so the reader cannot see what the author would change. A stronger version would focus on one or two lessons and show, for a specific exercise, how a different move would have altered the outcome.

@LINKS
The paper links the course's negotiation readings, from interest-based bargaining and difficult conversations to decision-making under emotion, to a series of practical exercises. Its clearest contribution is the observation that a negotiator's own assumptions and framing shape the outcome as much as the other party's position does.
