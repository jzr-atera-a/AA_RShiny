@OVERVIEW
The assignment examines a single decision from the point of view of a lending bank: should it offer cheaper green loans to H&M, a fashion retailer under pressure to cut emissions and waste? The paper frames the choice as a trade-off between reputational and regulatory gains on one side and higher default risk on the other. Part 1 sets out the bank's stakeholders, their positions on the policy, and a set of mitigations. Part 2 steps outside the bank and assesses the arguments of five other roles, including government, the CEO, an investor, a not-for-profit and a politician.

@BLOCK The bank's trade-off: cheaper green credit against higher default risk|Part 1, the lending decision
The central scenario is that the bank could cut the interest rate on loans to textile firms that meet green criteria, accepting a higher chance that a borrower fails. The paper sets out the regulatory context clearly, including the European Union's textile strategy and the growing reporting obligations on banks' green lending. It then classes stakeholders as primary (the corporate client, the board, shareholders and customers) and secondary (credit rating agencies, regulators and not-for-profit rankers). The most useful analytical step is to show how the same policy looks different to each: the board sees reputational and regulatory benefit, the CEO and CFO see a risk of greenwashing, and the two classes of shareholder disagree because of their time horizons.

@BLOCK Managing the risk without abandoning the policy|Mitigations and objectives
The response to that trade-off is a set of controls: benchmarking H&M against other textile clients, building a weighted average cost of capital model using market betas, and writing objectives and key results for the loan to 2035. The objectives include stress-testing risky loans, real-time monitoring of financial distress indicators, and a minimum net interest margin on high-risk lending. The paper is right that a policy of cheaper credit needs explicit guardrails, and the controls are the sort a credit committee would expect. The tension is that the margin target on high-risk loans sits uneasily beside a policy of reducing rates for green borrowers, and the paper does not show how both can hold at once.

@BLOCK Testing the other roles against the bank's view|Part 2, the other stakeholders
The second part examines the government, the CEO, an investor, a not-for-profit organisation and a politician. The most valuable contrasts are between the investor, who manages risk across a portfolio, and the bank, which assesses one borrower; and between the government's focus on carbon pricing and tax incentives and the NGO's focus on systemic change. The discussion of the textile sector is grounded in facts about the low share of clothing that is recycled, and in doubts about whether green funding schemes are monitored properly. These are useful points that the bank's own analysis could use.

@CRITIQUE
The paper is structured clearly and keeps the stakeholder perspective consistent, which is its main strength. Its analysis is qualitative where the decision needs numbers. The size of the rate reduction, the default probability at which the policy stops paying, and the expected reputational value are never estimated, so the conclusion that the bank should proceed with controls cannot be weighed against its cost. Part 2 is uneven: the CEO section criticises the quality of the CEO's material while relying on the same kind of secondary sourcing, and the politician section notes a bias towards the United States without correcting it. The cover sheet also gives the wrong assignment code. A stronger version would put a figure on the trade-off and state the threshold at which the green-lending policy would be withdrawn.

@LINKS
The paper is a practical example of the course's argument that a sustainability policy and a financial policy must be justified in the same terms. It shows how stakeholder analysis can expose the different time horizons and incentives that determine whether a green initiative is backed or resisted, and how a bank can keep a policy of concessionary lending while limiting the risk it creates.
