@OVERVIEW
This individual project report describes a placement at the Cambridge Centre for Alternative Finance, where the author built a methodology for estimating the energy use and cost of generative AI inference across cloud and specialist providers. It covers the project brief and context, the author's learning goals, the method (a three-track classification of data quality, a capacity-cost approach, and cross-provider comparison), validation, stakeholder work, and the author's professional development. It closes with a dashboard built to summarise the findings. The report argues that energy efficiency is becoming a material cost and reporting issue for financial firms that rely on AI.

@BLOCK Why AI energy use matters to alternative finance|Brief and context
The report sets the problem clearly. Inference, the running of models for users rather than their training, is said to account for most AI energy use over a deployment, and alternative finance firms rely on AI for credit scoring, fraud detection and trading. The link to reporting duties on cloud emissions gives the project a real purpose. The weakness is that several headline figures are stated as settled, including a projected cost range that appears in two different forms in the text, and per-query costs that depend on electricity prices the report does not state. These need sourcing and a stated price assumption.

@BLOCK A three-track method for uneven data|Methodology and data collection
The method is the report's main contribution. Providers are classified into three tracks depending on the quality of their disclosures: direct capacity figures, proxy indicators, or financial estimates. Data were gathered from more than fifty sources, and the report explains the difficulty of comparing disclosures across providers and jurisdictions. Regional construction-cost factors and power usage effectiveness are used to convert financial figures into energy estimates. The design is sensible, because it makes the uncertainty of each estimate visible rather than hiding it. The report does not, however, show the classification criteria, the conversion factors or the confidence bands in a table, so the method cannot be reproduced from the text.

@BLOCK Per-query energy and provider differences|Technical analysis and comparison
The results section gives per-query energy figures for different models and a comparison of providers, with claims such as a 15 to 20 per cent per-query advantage for one platform and a 25 to 40 per cent total-cost advantage for efficient providers. These are the most useful numbers in the report, and they are the most in need of checking. Most come from provider disclosures, sustainability reports and company blogs, which are measured by their publishers, not independently. The report describes validation within plus or minus 10 per cent for one provider, but the basis for that band is not explained. The comparison therefore shows what providers disclose, which is a different thing from what their systems use.

@BLOCK Validation, stakeholders and the author's development|Validation, engagement and learning goals
The validation section describes cross-checks between capital-expenditure, GPU-hour and benchmark approaches, which is the right instinct for an uncertain estimate. The stakeholder section describes working with the centre's team under limited direct feedback, and the report is candid that this was difficult. The learning objectives are stated in detail and linked to career goals in consulting and investment. Much of this material is about the author's development rather than the analysis, and it takes up a substantial share of the word count.

@BLOCK Future work and the case for real-time data|Future directions and conclusion
The report proposes real-time telemetry, automated data collection, Monte Carlo uncertainty bands, grid carbon intensity and cost forecasting as the next steps. These are the right priorities, because each would reduce the reliance on disclosures that the report identifies as the main limit. The conclusion argues that the project positions the centre to influence standards, although it does not describe any engagement with standards bodies or regulators.

@CRITIQUE
The report is clear about its problem and honest about the limits of disclosed data, and the three-track method is a useful way to organise uncertain evidence. Its main weakness is that the quantitative claims are not yet traceable. Several figures are attributed to sources that do not appear to support them: an estimate that inference could take 15 to 25 per cent of cloud spend is credited to a social media company's sustainability report, and a cost range is given in two different forms. Per-query energy and cost figures depend on electricity prices, but no price is stated. The validation band of plus or minus 10 per cent needs its method. The report also gives a great deal of space to the author's learning and career, which crowds out the analysis the reader came for. The dashboard is a helpful supporting tool, but the report should state the key assumptions in the text so that a reader can check the dashboard's outputs without logging in. The strongest revision would tabulate the method's inputs and outputs, give a price assumption for every cost figure, and mark which numbers are provider-reported and which are independently estimated.

@LINKS
The report applies the course's operations, finance and sustainability material to a measurement problem in a fast-moving technology market. Its most transferable lesson is that when disclosures are uneven, a method should make the uncertainty of each estimate explicit, and should separate what a provider reports from what can be independently verified.
