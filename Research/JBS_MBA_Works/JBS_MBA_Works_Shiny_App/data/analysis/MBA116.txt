@OVERVIEW
This group assignment for the Digital Business course sets out a strategy for Green Data, a hypothetical company whose driverless-car AI runs in large data centres, to make those data centres environmentally sustainable. The plan combines AI-driven efficiency, a shift to solar power, and an unusual data-centre design that includes underwater sites. The aim is net-zero emissions by 2030, with a commercial plan to 2034. The discussion is built around the company's stated purpose, the trade-off between AI's energy demand and its efficiency gains, and a set of proposed sites and partners.

@BLOCK A sustainability purpose for a data-driven company|Solution outline and the Massive Transformative Purpose
The solution sets out three strands: AI to reduce energy use and emissions, a move to renewable power led by a solar investment in southern England, and an efficient data-centre design that includes underwater sites and heat reuse. Green Data partners with ARM for low-power chips and with SAC for solar power, with a target of net-zero emissions by 2030. The paper frames this as a Massive Transformative Purpose, a stretch aim that can organise a fast-moving, data-driven company. The framing is persuasive, although a stretch target has value only if the plan shows a route to it, and the solution outline does not yet do that.

@BLOCK Balancing AI demand against its energy footprint|Environmental impact and the evidence used
The discussion holds two views of generative AI at once. On one side, AI can be less carbon-intensive than human work for some tasks, and it can cut data-centre energy use, as in the reported DeepMind cooling results. On the other, data-centre energy demand is expected to double, and training a large model may emit as much as several cars over their lifetimes. The paper also raises water use through a Chilean data-centre case. The balance is a good analytical move, because it shows that the sustainability case depends on what the AI is used for. The weakness is that the two sides rest on a mix of news, video and popular books rather than on data about Green Data's own likely demand.

@BLOCK Cooling, location and the proposed sites|Opportunities: solar, underwater and natural cooling
The paper looks for opportunities in location and cooling. It chooses a solar farm, adjusts the calculation for lower UK sunlight, and sets a target of about 70,000 tonnes of CO2 saved between 2024 and 2034. It then proposes placing a data centre underwater near a coastal solar farm, using Microsoft's Scottish trial as a reference, to gain natural cooling and shorter transmission. Meta's use of river water and its reinforcement-learning control of cooling are cited as further models. The ideas are concrete, and the Microsoft reference is a real precedent. However, the paper does not address maintenance, permitting or the cost of underwater deployment, and the 70,000-tonne figure is given without its working.

@BLOCK Chips, edge AI and the plan's conclusion|Electronic design and the conclusion
The final section argues that AI can run locally on devices to reduce energy use, and that ARM's low-power designs can be used in both the vehicles and the data centres. The conclusion draws these threads together as a plan to minimise environmental impact, especially for AI in driverless cars. The argument is coherent as an outline, but it links the company's product, driverless-car AI, to the sustainability goal mainly through the ARM partnership. It leaves the commercial case, which the introduction promised, largely undeveloped.

@CRITIQUE
The paper is ambitious and well referenced across the course material, and its treatment of the trade-off between AI's energy demand and its efficiency gains is its strongest analytical point. Its main weakness is that the plan is not yet a plan. The 70,000-tonne saving is stated without a calculation, the long-term commercial plan promised to 2034 is not set out, and the underwater data centre is proposed without any cost, risk or permitting discussion. The sustainability case also depends on partners whose involvement is assumed rather than tested. Several supporting facts come from popular sources, such as a video and a general AI book, and the link between the cited Meta and Microsoft examples and Green Data's own circumstances is asserted. The paper would be more persuasive if it modelled the energy use of one data centre under two designs, costed the underwater option against a conventional solar-plus-cooling option, and showed which assumption would cause the 2030 net-zero target to fail.

@LINKS
The paper illustrates the course's digital business argument that platform and ecosystem partnerships, here with a chip designer and a solar developer, can create value that a single firm could not. It also shows that a stretch purpose can shape a data-driven company's choices, and that the environmental cost of a digital business is part of its strategy rather than a separate issue.
