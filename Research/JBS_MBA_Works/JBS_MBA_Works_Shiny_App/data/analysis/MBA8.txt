@OVERVIEW
This reflective report follows one student's team through the Cambridge Venture Project, a consulting engagement for a charity. It is organised as a sequence of five incidents, each read through a management framework from the Praxis course. The central lesson is that the project's difficulties were largely interpersonal and motivational, and that the team recovered once the author changed how he led. The report ends with a measurable outcome: the team ran fifteen interviews against an objective of ten, and the client asked for further work.

@BLOCK Setting the frame: assumptions made before the team had met|Context and early assumptions
The author begins by recording four assumptions he made at the start: that the client wanted a business result, that a team from a leading business school would be highly committed, that the team shared a view of the client's needs, and that members would be motivated by the humanitarian purpose. The client visit and the first two weeks showed that the first assumption was wrong, and that the motivation of members varied widely. The author applies the project funnel and a task, group and individual needs model to structure what followed. The early choice to test assumptions openly is the report's most useful methodological point, because it turns the team's problems into things that could be checked rather than simply endured.

@BLOCK Confrontation, feedback and the cost of public correction|Incidents I to IV
The incidents show a series of interpersonal tensions. After a quiet client visit, the author challenged a reserved member privately about her silence, then invited her to speak in a group session. The approach was well meant, but a second team member felt she had been put on the spot, and the team disagreed about whether the intervention was right. A more serious conflict followed when a member openly refused to take on extra work, which the author handled by reframing the problem as shared and by facilitating a joint session. The author also describes a power struggle with his co-lead, whose instructions were sometimes contradictory. His response was to use one-to-one conversations and to adjust his own style, drawing on difficult-conversation guidance. The analysis here is candid about the author's own part in the friction.

@BLOCK From structure and ownership to a measured result|Incident V and outcomes
Once the team was disengaged, the author created a project plan with a shared tracker and a Gantt chart, and set objectives and key results on the model popularised at Intel and Google. He also adopted a principle of disagreeing in discussion but committing to a shared decision. The team exceeded its interview target, and the client praised the outputs and asked for more. The strongest evidence in the report is this outcome, because it shows that the change in working method had a visible effect. The report also notes that the client's advice on inclusive hiring and quantified outcomes was useful, although it does not describe how that advice was tested.

@CRITIQUE
The report is honest about its difficulties, and its reflective method is appropriate for a praxis module. It is less rigorous as analysis. The frameworks are named and applied quickly, but the report seldom asks whether they explain what happened, and several conclusions rest on single anecdotes. The author presents himself as the rational leader who adapts, while the account of his own interventions, such as the public request to a quiet member, is mainly defended rather than examined. The report also names team members in incidents where anonymity would have been more appropriate. A stronger version would ask what structural issues, such as unclear roles, unequal motivation or the lack of a client sponsor, produced the conflicts, and would test whether the chosen interventions were the only reasonable ones.

@LINKS
The report is a clear example of the course's point that teams are shaped by unstated assumptions, and that good praxis means making those assumptions explicit and testing them. It also shows how management ideas from very different sources, from research on decision making to popular business books, are used in practice when a team is under pressure.
