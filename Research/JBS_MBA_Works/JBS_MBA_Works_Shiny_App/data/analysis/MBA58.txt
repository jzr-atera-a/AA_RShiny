@OVERVIEW
This group assignment for New Venture Finance values three ventures, Alpha, Beta and Gamma, and recommends how to split a small investment of £150,000 across them. Each venture is valued with a version of the venture capital method: a projected exit value in five years is discounted to today at a target rate of return, then reduced to allow for the share of the company that will be given to later investors. The valuations are then used to compare expected returns at different exit multiples, and to justify an allocation of £60,000 to Alpha, £50,000 to Beta and £40,000 to Gamma.

@BLOCK Three ventures and the price each one asks|Starting valuations
The paper starts from the price per share offered by each venture and converts it into a pre-money and post-money valuation. Alpha, an energy business, is priced at £4.25 a share, giving a pre-money value of about £17.9 million. Beta, a medical device company, is priced at £0.90 a share, about £4.2 million before the round. Gamma, which competes in a UK market worth about £900 million, is priced at £2.80 a share, about £7.4 million. These starting points set up the central question for each venture: whether the exit value the team projects, after allowing for the return an investor requires and for later dilution, comes out above the price being asked. The arithmetic here is straightforward, and each step is shown.

@BLOCK Alpha: a mature venture valued on its five-year exit|Discounted exit and dilution for Alpha
For Alpha the team projects year-five revenue of £27.3 million and EBITDA of £12.8 million, and applies industry multiples to produce an exit range of about £109 million to £115 million. Discounting the upper figure at 20 per cent over five years gives about £46.2 million, and allowing for 2.2 per cent of equity transferred to angel investors gives an implied value of about £45.2 million. The discounting itself is calculated correctly. The weakness lies in the inputs: the valuation rests on a single set of projections, and the 20 per cent rate is described as suitable for a mid-sized energy company without any test of how the rate should change with the risk of this particular business. The implied value is also far above the price being asked, and the paper does not explain why the market price is so much lower.

@BLOCK Beta and Gamma: early ventures, higher rates and heavier dilution|Discounting the exits of the two riskier ventures
Beta is valued on an exit of £50 million with a 30 per cent discount rate. The rate is justified by industry benchmarks for mid-stage medtech and by evidence of traction, including 76 customers and three new hospital clients a month. The discounted value is about £13.5 million, and after future dilution of 38.6 per cent it falls to about £8.3 million. Gamma is the most detailed case. The team estimates market growth from 2012 to 2017, projects the UK market five years ahead, and tests market shares of 1, 2 and 3 per cent. It applies a 3x revenue multiple and a 45 per cent venture discount rate, and caps future dilution at 30 per cent. The scenario logic is useful because it shows how sensitive the value is to market share, which is the key assumption for a company of this kind.

@BLOCK Returns at different exit multiples and the final allocation|Returns table and recommendation
The returns table compares the three ventures at 3x, 5x and 10x exit multiples. Gamma offers the highest potential return and the highest dilution risk, Beta offers strong upside with substantial ownership but heavy future funding needs, and Alpha offers modest returns with a more established position and lower risk. The group recommends a balanced portfolio of £60,000 in Alpha, £50,000 in Beta and £40,000 in Gamma. The logic is sound as a risk argument: a larger commitment to the most mature company limits the downside, while the smaller allocation to Gamma recognises its dilution risk. The recommendation is clearer than the numbers that support it, and that is where the paper is weakest.

@CRITIQUE
The paper's strength is its method. It sets out each step of the valuation, the dilution adjustment and the scenario logic in a form a reader can follow and check, and it presents its allocation with a clear rationale. Its weaknesses lie in the arithmetic and in consistency. Gamma's five-year market figure does not follow from the stated growth rate: 3.7 per cent a year applied to £900 million gives about £1.08 billion, not the £1.3 billion used in the scenarios. Discounting £39 million at 45 per cent over five years gives about £6.1 million rather than £7.4 million, and the owners' £10.7 million discounted to today is about £1.7 million, not the £2.03 million reported. Beta's exit is given as £50 million in one place and as £54.9 million and £47.9 million in the table. The ownership stakes in the returns table also cannot be reconciled with the sums invested: £50,000 at a post-money value of about £5 million buys about 1 per cent, not 16 per cent. The paper uses a different discount rate for each venture without a common basis, so the ranking is largely a product of those choices. A stronger version would use one rate framework, show a sensitivity table for the main assumption in each case, and check every figure against its source before the recommendation is made.

@LINKS
The paper applies the venture capital method that the course uses to value early-stage companies: estimate a future exit, discount it at a return that reflects risk, and adjust for dilution from later rounds. It is a practical illustration of how the choice of discount rate and exit assumption can drive an investment decision more than the headline valuation does.
