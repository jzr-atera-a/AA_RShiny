@OVERVIEW
This individual assessment asks whether, for a retail mutual fund choosing between polluting and low-pollution US firms, pollution is better treated as a risk factor or as a behavioural factor in pricing. The paper concludes that it is mainly a risk factor, because polluting firms face regulatory, carbon and transition costs that justify higher expected returns. It builds the case in three strands: the growth of regulation and sustainability rankings, the role of diversification and factor models in mutual funds, and the Fama-French framework for explaining returns.

@BLOCK Sustainability as a pressure on costs and prices|Regulation and rankings
The discussion opens with the argument that firms which prioritise sustainability face higher operating costs, from cleaner technology and stricter reporting, and that rankings such as Corporate Knights give investors a way to identify lower-risk companies. The section on US firms adds the expected climate disclosure rules and the pressure on high-emission firms from ESG-screened funds. The point that matters for the question is that pollution affects prices through expected future costs such as litigation, carbon taxes and divestment. This is a reasonable reading of the evidence. It explains why pollution could be priced, but not whether the market has priced it through risk or through investor preference.

@BLOCK What a retail fund can and cannot do with the risk|Diversification, behaviour and factor models
The mutual fund section explains that diversification removes unsystematic risk and that behavioural biases can cause mispricing. It then argues that a fund can address pollution risk by tilting towards low-carbon firms and using a factor model. This is a sensible practical conclusion, but the paper does not show how such a tilt would perform in the simulated scenario, or what it would cost in return. The treatment of behavioural finance is brief. The paper acknowledges that sentiment and ESG-driven demand can push low-pollution stocks into overvaluation, which is itself a behavioural explanation, yet it does not return to that point when it reaches its conclusion.

@BLOCK Reading the return gap as compensation for risk|Pollution as a risk factor
The core argument is that high-pollution firms earn higher expected returns because investors demand compensation for regulatory, climate and transition risk, and that the Fama-French three-factor model is the right framework for thinking about this compensation. The model is used to suggest that risk, rather than bias, explains the returns. The argument is coherent, but the paper relies on the model to settle the question rather than testing whether pollution adds explanatory power beyond market, size and value. The three-factor model was not designed to test environmental exposure, so using it as the decisive framework needs more justification.

@CRITIQUE
The paper is clearly written, and its conclusion follows a consistent line from regulation to pricing to portfolio choice. Its main weakness is that the question is answered by argument rather than by evidence. The scenario data for the polluting and clean portfolios are described but not analysed in the text, so the reader cannot see what return gap the scenario produces, or how much of it survives once known factors are taken into account. The strongest counter-evidence is in the reading list but is not used. The Pastor, Stambaugh and Taylor paper on sustainable investing in equilibrium argues that investors' preference for green assets can lower the expected return of those assets, which is a preference-based channel that would weaken the paper's conclusion. Treating the risk and behavioural explanations as rivals, rather than as parts of the same market, would make the verdict more convincing. The introduction also promises a comparison of polluting and non-polluting firms that the body does not deliver in numbers.

@LINKS
The paper ties the course's finance material to a sustainability question, and it shows how factor models, diversification and regulatory expectations combine when a retail fund prices environmental exposure. Its contribution is a clear framework for the risk view. Its limit is that the risk and behavioural explanations are presented as alternatives, when in practice a green premium created by investor preference would also look like a risk factor in the data.
