@OVERVIEW
This individual assignment examines the governance and ethics of Meta Platforms, the owner of Facebook, Instagram and WhatsApp. It is prompted by two developments: a round of layoffs at the same time as large executive pay increases, and the company's growing use of generative AI. The paper covers board leadership and ownership, compensation, employee relations, stakeholder engagement, ethical frameworks for AI, and internal and external governance controls. It closes with recommendations on board independence, executive pay tied to long-term outcomes, and stakeholder communication.

@BLOCK Founder control and the limits of accountability|Board leadership and compensation
The paper's central governance finding is that Meta's dual-class share structure gives its founder far more voting power than public shareholders, which in turn limits the board's accountability. The author connects this to the wider literature on power consolidation, and to reported approval of executive bonuses of up to 200 per cent of salary during a period of mass layoffs. The argument that pay should be tied to long-term outcomes, and that OKRs could make board goals measurable, is a sensible way to link incentives to the company's stated mission. The analysis is clearest where it identifies the conflict between a pay decision and the job cuts that accompanied it.

@BLOCK Layoffs, talent and the trust of the workforce|Employee relations and stakeholder engagement
The employee section argues that the layoffs weaken the company's commitment to workforce development, and that remaining staff need retraining, open communication and wellbeing support to stay engaged. The stakeholder section links this to fairness: job cuts alongside executive rewards undermine trust. The strongest point is the connection between perceived fairness and retention, since a workforce that sees pay decisions as unfair is less likely to commit to change. The recommendations, including a communication plan with defined audiences and channels, are general, and the paper does not show how Meta would measure whether trust had improved.

@BLOCK Privacy, moderation and AI as governance problems|Social and ethical issues from data and AI
This section reviews the Cambridge Analytica case, algorithmic bias in news feeds, the limits of AI-based moderation and the risks of generative AI. It draws on a range of academic and popular sources to argue that the pace of AI development can outrun governance. Its insight is that these are governance issues rather than purely technical ones, because decisions about moderation and data use are made by the board and senior management. The section would be stronger if it tied each issue to a specific control, such as an audit of moderation error rates, rather than listing risks in sequence.

@BLOCK Three ethical lenses and a set of controls|Ethical frameworks and governance controls
The paper applies three frameworks from the course: authenticity, normative and behavioural. It argues that Meta should track its own responses to misinformation, follow AI governance standards with independent oversight, and design AI around user benefit and AI literacy. It then sets out internal controls (board oversight, internal audit, linked executive pay and compliance) and external controls (shareholder activism, regulation, media and independent audit). This is the most complete part of the paper, because it moves from diagnosis to a list of mechanisms a reader can assess.

@CRITIQUE
The paper identifies the right problems and reaches a coherent set of recommendations, but its central tension is not resolved. It establishes that founder control through dual-class shares limits accountability, then recommends greater board independence, shareholder engagement and independent audit without explaining how these could be imposed on a company whose founder controls the votes. Any credible answer would have to address that constraint directly, for example by proposing controls that do not depend on the board's composition, such as disclosure requirements or pay linked to measures of employee welfare. The analysis also relies heavily on press reports for its key facts, several of which are undated or cited inconsistently, and on general management books for its frameworks, so the link between evidence and recommendation has to be taken on trust. The claim that the 2023 restructuring produced good long-term performance is asserted without a source. The paper is most useful when it turns the frameworks into controls, and it would be stronger if it did this for each of the three ethical lenses in turn.

@LINKS
The paper applies the course's governance themes to a company whose ownership structure makes ordinary accountability hard. It shows how the choice between shareholder value and stakeholder responsibility appears in concrete decisions about pay and jobs, and that an ethical framework is only useful when it leads to a control that someone is responsible for operating.
