@OVERVIEW
This group presentation for BlackRock asks how the firm should adopt AI in real estate investment. It moves from the history of real estate cycles and the evolution of AI, to sector-level effects, to the data and workflow where AI adds value, to a method for making AI output decision-grade, and finally to risks and a three-horizon roadmap. The deck argues that AI is a fourth wave that reshapes demand, liquidity and exit, and that the firms which win will be those able to make AI judgements explainable enough for clients to accept and price.

@BLOCK AI as the fourth wave in real estate|Evolution, trends and sector effects
The deck places AI alongside earlier waves of real estate change, from industrialisation and post-war suburbanisation to the computer age and the shift caused by Covid. It then sorts asset classes into front runners (data centres, logistics and life sciences), marginal gainers (hotels, living sectors and senior living) and stragglers (office and retail). The most decision-relevant output is this sector ranking, although it is largely qualitative. The reasons given for data centres and life sciences are demand-side, and the deck does not measure how much of the sector movement AI causes, as opposed to power supply, demographics or interest rates. The "dual edge" argument, that AI sharpens institutional investors while weakening public finances and retail demand, is a real second-order effect that the deck mentions only briefly.

@BLOCK Where AI adds value in the deal workflow|Data, deal analysis and case evidence
The deck sets out data sources, from proprietary transaction records to social media, academic work and open government portals, and maps AI applications onto each, from anomaly detection and missing-data prediction to synthetic data for thin histories. It then walks through a deal workflow from sourcing to asset management, with AI document review, market sentiment analysis and lease abstraction at each stage. The cases are useful: one lease review covered 100 tenant leases in ten days and found more than twenty missing documents, and another firm used sentiment analysis as an early signal of market change. However, most cases come from vendors, press coverage or summaries, and the sovereign fund's productivity figures are targets rather than outcomes. The message that the better the data, the better the AI is the deck's most important practical point. The proposed use of synthetic data to fill gaps carries a risk the deck flags only briefly: synthetic data trained on thin histories can reproduce the bias it is meant to correct.

@BLOCK Making AI judgement explainable enough to price|Structural intelligence and the verification loop
The deck's most original contribution is to redefine explainability as priceability: the question is not how a model works, but whether a client will buy its answer. It proposes a three-step structural intelligence process, combining data augmentation with a hybrid of general and quantitative models, and a loop that checks each assumption and sends failures back to the model. A demonstration covers a data-centre exit through a real estate investment trust, with a stated assumption set and an expected IRR of 15 per cent. The idea is persuasive as a design principle. The demo is illustrative rather than demonstrated, however: the deck does not show how the verification would produce the 15 per cent figure, and it does not justify the 60 per cent confidence threshold that triggers a re-run.

@BLOCK Risks, guardrails and the three-horizon roadmap|Risks, safeguards and recommendations
The risk register rates GenAI risks as minor, moderate or high, with a mitigation for each. The guardrail section is practical and honest about trade-offs: a prompt-level rule, a stop list and a trained classifier each have different precision, and the deck recommends combining them to address fair-housing rules. The roadmap has three horizons. The first, in the next year, covers consolidated real estate data, AI in underwriting and due diligence, and productivity tools for investment teams. The second, over two to five years, covers a scalable platform, talent, and portfolio-level optimisation. The third, beyond five years, covers AI-centric assets and new ownership models such as tokenised real estate. The sequencing is sensible. Two recommendations pull against the expert interviews, however. Experts advised against building custom models, which become obsolete quickly, yet the plan proposes a proprietary real estate language model in the second horizon. The first-horizon plan to equip teams with a general chatbot also sits uneasily with the deck's own argument that AI outputs must be explainable.

@BLOCK Expert views and what they add|Interview evidence
The six interviews are the deck's most grounded evidence. Their caveats are useful and sometimes under-used in the main argument. One expert prefers decision trees because they rank features, and warns that generative models often miss critical details. Another says generative opacity is a legal risk in sensitive decisions, though safer for asset investment. A third argues that AI may not accelerate trends but amplifies value in well-managed assets, and that custom tools go out of date quickly. Several note that data quality and training matter more than new model architectures. These points would make the recommendations more credible if they were reflected in the roadmap.

@CRITIQUE
The deck is ambitious and clearly organised, with a strong narrative arc from history to roadmap. Its strongest contributions are the reframing of explainability as priceability and its attention to data quality. Its weaknesses are evidential. Most of the quantitative claims, including the sector rankings, the efficiency gains and the productivity targets, come from vendors, press or case summaries, and the deck does not separate what AI causes from what it merely accompanies. The verification demo is illustrative rather than demonstrated. The recommendations are sequenced but not costed: the deck asks BlackRock to build a central real estate database in the first year without estimating cost, staffing or time. The reference list is long, but many entries sit in the appendix without being linked to specific claims. A stronger version would separate causal from coincident effects, show the verification logic in a worked example, and reconcile the roadmap with the experts' cautions about custom models and interpretability.

@LINKS
The deck applies the course's digital real estate themes: data as the constraint on AI value, changes to workflow and platforms, and the governance needed for AI in investment decisions. Its explainability argument connects directly to the course's broader point that a technology is only useful to a firm when its outputs can be defended to the people who fund it.
