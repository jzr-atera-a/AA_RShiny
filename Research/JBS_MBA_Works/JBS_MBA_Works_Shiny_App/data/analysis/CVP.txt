@OVERVIEW
This group presentation for the Cambridge Venture Project examines why Arthur Rank Hospice Charity (ARHC), the only adult hospice serving people with life-limiting illness in Cambridgeshire, reaches so few non-White European patients. Using the hospice's own patient report for April 2023 to September 2024, it shows that non-White European residents make up about 12 per cent of the county but only about 3.5 per cent of patients. The team combines that evidence with secondary research and interviews, distils the barriers into a set of decision factors, and recommends outreach, language support, workforce diversity and simpler referral, followed by a risk register and an action plan.

@BLOCK A charity whose reach is narrower than its community|Context and the measured gap
ARHC was founded in 1981, has 22 in-patient beds, serves about 3,600 patients a year, and is funded 60 per cent by the NHS and 40 per cent by retail and donors. That funding mix matters: a gap in who uses the hospice is also a gap in who supports it. The problem slide measures the gap directly. White British residents are over-represented among patients (about 90 per cent against about 82 per cent of the county), while every minority group is under-represented. Indian residents are about 2.3 per cent of the population but under 1 per cent of patients; residents of Other Mixed heritage are about 2.7 per cent of the population and about 0.1 per cent of patients. No minority group reaches half its population share, which shows the issue is systemic rather than confined to one community.

@BLOCK Why families and patients hold back|Secondary research: constraints
The research groups the barriers into religion and family values, demographic and geographic factors, communication, pain outcomes and decision-making. The most useful observation is that the barriers interact. A belief that God decides when death comes, mistrust after past experiences of racism or insensitivity, and a preference for caring at home can each lead a family to avoid disclosure, delay advance care planning or rely on relatives as interpreters, and those choices can worsen pain control when pain is under-reported. The hospice's own data adds a striking point here: religion is recorded as unknown for 58.7 per cent of patients, and Muslim, Hindu, Jewish and Buddhist patients together make up about 1 per cent. The hospice is trying to remove a faith barrier it largely cannot see in its records.

@BLOCK What outreach elsewhere teaches, and what the interviews added|Results and insights
The outreach best practice draws mainly on a US guide for Black and African American communities: build partnerships with community organisations, work through faith leaders, celebrate life rather than only end of life, and engage communities directly rather than through media. The ideas transfer in principle, though the US system is Medicare-driven while the UK model is NHS-integrated; the comparison table rates the UK at 80 per cent accessibility and 85 per cent integration against 85 and 90 for the US. The interviews give the most local findings: no defined process for referred patients, access problems in rural areas, bureaucracy around Hospice at Home, and a fear that hospice means only end-of-life care. The hospice's records also show that 51.9 per cent of its patients die at home and 40.5 per cent in the hospice, so the at-home service is central to any outreach to families who prefer to care at home.

@BLOCK From barriers to a decision model and a plan|Conclusions, recommendations, risks and actions
The conclusion turns the barriers into the factors that shape a family's choice: perception of hospice, cultural and religious beliefs, family support, language, location and services, and past experience of health care. The recommendations follow the patient journey from awareness to engagement to intake: early-stage awareness campaigns and personal stories; relationships with religious and community leaders; inclusive hiring, cultural competency training and translators; and, with the NHS, simpler referral and services in rural areas. The risk register rates cultural misconceptions as the only high-impact risk, lack of trust as medium, and recruitment and paperwork as low. The action plan puts trust-building first (campaigns, training, community leaders) and structural change later (referral redesign, satellite services, continuous evaluation).

@CRITIQUE
The deck is clearly structured, and its strongest feature is that it measures the problem with the hospice's own data rather than asserting it. Its weaknesses are in what it does with that evidence. The interviews, as the team acknowledges, were mainly with White interviewees, so the barriers minority families face are reported at second hand, largely from US and national sources. The unknown-religion figure of 58.7 per cent is shown but not acted on: better recording of ethnicity, faith and language is the obvious first recommendation and is missing. The decision factors are not ranked, so the plan cannot say which barrier matters most. The comparison of hospice models gives accessibility and integration percentages without saying how they were measured. Finally, no action has an owner, cost or target; a measurable goal, such as raising the minority share of referrals from about 3.5 per cent towards the county's 12 per cent, would let the hospice see whether the plan works.

@LINKS
The deck applies the course's consulting method: measure the problem, combine secondary evidence with stakeholder interviews, and turn the findings into prioritised recommendations with a risk plan. It also shows the course's point that a charity must reconcile its mission with a funding model that depends on public goodwill, because widening access costs money that the charity has to raise.
