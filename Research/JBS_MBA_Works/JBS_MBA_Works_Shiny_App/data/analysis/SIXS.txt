@OVERVIEW
This Lean Six Sigma project presentation, for the Centre for Endowment Asset Management (CEAM), describes how the author used the DMADV method to design a forex spot price analysis platform in Python and R-Shiny for several terabytes of foreign exchange trading data. The slides carry the project's numbers in their panels: quality targets, a time study of how researchers spend 33 analytical hours a week, the usage and effectiveness of current validation methods, root causes, a 15-week build plan and accuracy standards of 99.5 to 99.9 per cent. The conclusion is that a research-grade platform must put accuracy ahead of speed.

@BLOCK A data problem defined by its users|Define: voice of the customer and quality targets
The project starts from interviews with CEAM's research students, who asked for clean datasets with transparent outlier removal, descriptive statistics and volatility measures, correlation heatmaps, flexible filters by date, currency pair and frequency, and export to Python and MATLAB. These needs become four quality targets: 95 per cent for data cleaning, 92 per cent for outlier detection accuracy, 99 per cent for statistical rigour and 85 per cent for visual clarity. Defining requirements in the users' own words before designing anything is the method's main strength, and it is the right choice because the platform did not exist before. DMADV, which designs new processes, fits the task better than DMAIC, which improves existing ones.

@BLOCK Measuring the current workflow before changing it|Measure: Gage R&R and the time study
The measure phase does two things. A Gage R&R design (10 parts, 3 operators, 3 measurements each, judged against bands of under 10 per cent good, 10 to 30 marginal and over 30 poor) checks whether data from Bloomberg, Reuters and internal systems can be measured consistently. A time study then shows where effort goes: 65 per cent on working out the analytical method, 24 per cent on understanding data structures, 18 per cent on outlier detection, 12 per cent on validation and 9 per cent on documentation, within about 33 analytical hours a week. The data itself is in good shape, 99.2 per cent complete with a 2.3 per cent outlier rate. The most telling finding is about validation: visual inspection of price plots is used 95 per cent of the time but is 85 per cent effective, while statistical review is used less (88 per cent) yet is more effective (92 per cent). The current process relies most on its weakest check.

@BLOCK From symptoms to root causes|Analyse: root cause analysis and tool gaps
Five whys and a fishbone diagram trace the problem, that Python and MATLAB work on small samples but fail at terabyte scale, to three root causes: no real-time visualisation, poor data aggregation and memory handling, and a monolithic, single-threaded architecture. Each cause is paired with a fix: real-time dashboards, chunked data processing and distributed computing. The tool assessment adds that the existing tools are statistically capable, so the gap is in workflow, automation and collaboration rather than in analytical power. This is a precise diagnosis, and it explains why the solution is a platform rather than new statistical methods.

@BLOCK Designing the platform and setting the bar for proof|Design and Verify: experiments, build plan and accuracy standards
Design of experiments is used to choose frequency settings, outlier thresholds and chart parameters. The architecture covers multi-frequency data, automatic USD alignment, built-in outlier detection, risk analytics such as VaR and Sharpe ratios, and export back to Python and R. The build runs for 15 weeks in three phases: data infrastructure (weeks 1 to 4), the visualisation layer and dashboard (5 to 10) and integration (11 to 15). Verification sets accuracy standards of 99.8 per cent for statistics, 99.5 for volatility, 99.9 for correlation and 99.7 for returns, all above a 99.0 per cent industry benchmark, and uses control charts on dashboard complexity and time to insight. In practice, this is what the '100 per cent accuracy requirement' means: tolerances of a few tenths of a percentage point on specific outputs.

@CRITIQUE
The project is well structured and the slides are rich in metrics: the validation usage-versus-effectiveness comparison and the root-cause chain are strong pieces of analysis. The weaknesses are in consistency and in proof. The time-allocation shares add up to 128 per cent, so they cannot be read as a baseline without correction, and the hours (33 a week, of which 8 on data structure and 4 on validation) do not match the percentages. The deck sets targets and standards but shows no measured result after the platform was built: there is no before-and-after time comparison, no Gage R&R percentage, no list of the experiment's factor levels and no control limits that can be read. Several slide images are illustrative rather than data. The claim of '100 per cent accuracy' should be restated as the tolerances the deck already gives. A stronger version would add one measured outcome, for example hours per week spent on cleaning before and after, and the Gage R&R result.

@LINKS
The project applies the course's process improvement tools to software for research, showing that Six Sigma can design and validate a data platform as well as improve a physical process. Its clearest lesson is that the most-used control is not always the most effective one, so a redesign should start by measuring how well each existing check works.
